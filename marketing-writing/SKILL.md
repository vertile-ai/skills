# Marketing Writing

Promotional copy for landing pages, product intros, tutoring or sales promo, emails, and social posts. Not for instructional or assessment writing, where limitations often belong in the lesson.

## Core principle

Marketing copy is a press conference for a real offer, not a project diary and not a hype reel. Find the strongest advantage you can actually support, lead with it, and say it plainly. Be persuasive, not defensive. Be specific, not inflated. Never invent proof.

## Before you write

Diagnose the job, then change only as much as it requires:

- **Blank brief or generic draft:** build the argument from the brief and known facts. Latitude on angle, never on facts.
- **A draft with a real voice or angle:** keep that argument and vocabulary. Fix the buried lead, vague lines, AI tells, and structure.
- **A point edit** ("fix this headline"): change only what was asked, plus the minimum around it.

Gather, or ask for: the audience and their problem, the offer and what makes it different, the one primary action, and any real proof (numbers, quotes, policies). If a writing sample is provided, match its sentence length, punctuation, and word choice.

Run every line through three checks: can the reader picture it; can the claim be proven true or false; could a competitor write this exact sentence (if yes, rewrite or flag `[NEED: differentiator]`).

Hero spine: name the current discomfort in the reader's words, show a clearer better state, and make this CTA the believable path between them. Then prefix the headline with "Now you can…". Keep it only if the result is both concrete and true. "Now you can have a powerful platform" fails; "Now you can see which topic cost you marks" works.

## Language rules

**Lead with the strongest point.** Open with the problem, the distinctive offer, and the strongest supportable outcome. Do not open with process, background, or a tour of features.

**State the advantage explicitly.** Say who it is for, what changes for them, and why that is worth acting on now.

**No process diary.** Do not narrate how the copy, the product, or the team got here. Keep the standing logic: the problem, why existing options fall short, what you offer, and the proof.

**Do not volunteer weakness.** Do not lead with limitations, apologies, or a case against the offer. Do not compete on a dimension that is not a strength; choose the frame you can honestly win. If a limit must be stated (price, scope, eligibility), put it after the value is clear, as a precise fact, not as a confession.

**Banned self-weakening phrases.** Do not write: unfortunately, merely, only achieves, still lags, limited, just a, we're not perfect, we're still early, we can't yet, sorry if, it may not be for everyone (as an opener). Do not use hedges that drain a true claim: almost, very, really, quite, somewhat, arguably, might just, tries to, aims to (when the product already does the thing).

**Be direct and specific.** Short, active, concrete sentences. Benefits over features. One idea per section. Customer's language, not internal jargon. Prefer "use" to "utilize," "help" to "facilitate." Plain lines carry the page; reserve punch for the headline and at most one other moment.

**Cut AI-slop.** State the point; do not stage it, announce it, or dress it up.

Never use these patterns:

- Contrast reveals: "It's not X, it's Y," "not just X, but Y," "no X, no Y, no Z." State what is true. A single factual absence near a CTA is fine ("No card required").
- Throat-clearing: "Here's the thing," "Here's what you need to know," "Let's dive in," "The truth is," "Let me be clear," "Look," "Honestly?"
- Emphasis crutches: "Let that sink in," "Full stop," "Make no mistake," "This matters because," a one-line closer that only restates the paragraph.
- Stock marketing phrases: "In today's fast-paced world," "In a world where," "Whether you're X or Y," "Unlock the power of," "Say goodbye to," "X, reimagined," "Take it to the next level," "At its core," "When it comes to," "It's worth noting."
- Self-answered labels: "The result? 3x faster." "The best part: it learns." Just state the result. A question in the reader's voice is fine ("Tired of chasing approvals?").
- Inflated or empty words: game-changer, seamless, robust, powerful, innovative, streamline, optimize, utilize, groundbreaking, unlock, delve, tapestry, landscape (abstract), pivotal, testament, foster, showcase, leverage (as decoration).
- Fake authority: "experts say," "studies show," "industry-leading," "best-in-class," "trusted by thousands," unless you can name the source or the real number.

Keep rare: em dashes in headlines, subject lines, ads, and short social posts (use none there); stacks of fragments; forced lists of three. Vary sentence length. Do not bold every label. Do not end every section with a tidy closer.

Channel-specific tells: no fake "Re:" or "Fwd:" in subject lines; no "I hope this email finds you well," "Just checking in," or "Feel free to reach out if you have any questions." No broetry (one short sentence per line for the whole post), no "Agree?" / "Thoughts?" bait, no manufactured vulnerability, no emoji-as-bullets.

## Claims vs evidence

Persuasive is not the same as deceptive. Write only claims the available material supports.

- Never invent statistics, testimonials, customer names, case results, awards, rankings, certifications, or guarantees.
- A claim should be specific enough to be checked. "Cut weekly reporting from 4 hours to 15 minutes" is a claim; "best-in-class workflow" is decoration.
- If a requested claim is not supported, do not write it and do not silently drop it. Name the claim, say why it falls short, offer the closest true alternative, and state what evidence would unlock the original. Flag gaps as `[NEED: ...]`.
- Narrow a claim rather than stretch it. "Used by 12 local schools" beats "transforming education nationwide" if that is all you can support.

**Proof hierarchy** (use the highest level you actually have; leave a placeholder rather than invent):

1. First-party results with a name, metric, and context.
2. Attributed testimonials (person, role, situation).
3. Verifiable third-party proof (review score with count, certification, a named outlet and what it actually said).
4. Demonstrable product behavior (what happens after the click).
5. A real policy (trial, refund, cancel-anytime) stated in the same words the policy uses.
6. A visible `[NEED: proof]` slot.

Place proof next to the claim and near the CTA. Logos: recognizable beats numerous.

## Weak material: decision order

When a line, section, or requested claim is weak, handle it in this order:

1. **Cut** anything that does not serve the core claim.
2. **Narrow** the claim to what the evidence supports.
3. **Reframe** around a dimension you can honestly win (a different audience, job, constraint, or outcome).
4. **Flag or cut** the line (`[NEED: proof]` or `[NEED: differentiator]`) rather than decorate it.
5. **State a precise limit** only when it is unavoidable and after the value is clear.

Do not manufacture objections nobody raised. Do not enlarge the attack surface to look thorough.

## Voice and tone

Set these before drafting, and keep them consistent:

- **Formality:** casual / professional-friendly / formal.
- **Personality:** playful or serious; bold or understated; technical or accessible.
- **Intensity:** headlines may be bolder; body copy should be clearer; CTAs should be about the action.
- **Person:** prefer "you" for the reader and a consistent "I" or "we" for the speaker. Do not drift from "we" to "the company."

A writing sample beats the default. Keep their vocabulary ("stuff," "ship") instead of upgrading it to "solutions" and "deploy." Where they spent words is a priority; what they left out is a scope choice. Read it aloud: if a tone shift would sound like a different person, fix it.

## Headline and CTA bank

Formulas are starting shapes. Fill every slot with a specific, supportable fact, then run "Now you can…".

### Headlines

- Outcome: "{Achieve outcome} without {pain}"; "{Achieve outcome} by {how}"; "Turn {input} into {outcome}"; "{Outcome} in {timeframe}."
- Problem: "Never {unpleasant event} again"; a question that names the pain; "Stop {pain}. Start {better state}."
- Audience: "The {category} for {audience}"; "You don't have to {skill or resource} to {outcome}."
- Differentiation: "The {category} that {real differentiator}"; "The simple way to {outcome}."
- Proof (only with a real number): "{Number} {people} use {product} to {outcome}."

Offer 2–3 headline options with a one-line reason each when writing a page, not when doing a point edit.

### CTAs

- Formula: [action verb] + [what they get] + [qualifier if needed]. "Start my free trial." "Get the checklist." "See pricing for my team."
- Avoid empty labels: Submit, Sign Up, Learn More, Click Here. Use "Get started" only if nothing more specific fits.
- One primary CTA, visible without scrolling, repeated at decision points. A secondary CTA should be quieter and genuinely secondary (e.g. "See a sample" beside "Start free").
- Button text names the outcome, not the mechanics of clicking.

## Page, email, and social structure

**Pages.** Homepage: broadest true value, with paths for different intents. Landing page: one message, one CTA, a complete argument, matched to the traffic source. Feature page: feature → benefit → outcome, plus a use case. Pricing: help them choose; make the recommended plan obvious. About: why you exist, tied to a customer benefit, still with a CTA.

A visitor should understand what this is and why it matters within a few seconds. Prefer a short narrative over a feature list: hero, social-proof bar, problem in the reader's language, how it works (three or four concrete steps), three to five benefits, objection handling, final CTA that recaps value and adds a real risk reversal if you have one. Use only the sections the page needs. A compact ad landing page can stop at hero, proof, three benefits, one testimonial, how it works, and a final CTA.

**Email.** One email, one job, one primary CTA. Lead with usefulness; earn the ask. Subject: clear over clever, specific over vague, about 40–60 characters; offer 2–3 options. Preview text (~90–140 characters) extends the subject; do not repeat it. Body: hook, why it matters to them, the value, the CTA, a human sign-off. Short paragraphs, mobile-first. Rough length: 50–125 words transactional, 150–300 educational, longer only when a real story needs it. Do not invent urgency or discounts. If asked for a sequence: welcome or onboarding is a short series (deliver the promise first, then a quick win, proof, one objection, then the ask); delays of a day or two early on, longer later; each email still has one job.

**Social.** The first line is the hook; fill it with a fact, result, or real story. Useful shapes: "I was wrong about {belief}." "How to {outcome} without {pain}." "{Number} {things} that {outcome}." "I stopped {common practice} and {real result}." Match the platform: LinkedIn can be a short professional argument with paragraph breaks; X should be punchy and within the character limit; Instagram and short video are visual-first, with the point in the first seconds. End on the point or a question you actually want answered. Do not pad with hashtag walls or unearned emoji.

## Objections, proof, and honest persuasion

Use persuasion to make a true offer easier to accept, not to trap the reader.

**Name the job, not the feature.** People hire a product to get a job done. Frame the outcome they want.

**Objections.** Typical ones: price or value, "will this work for my situation?", effort or time, and "what if it doesn't work?" Answer the real ones near the CTA with a fact (who it is for, what setup takes, what the policy is). Do not invent a fear so you can soothe it. Do not hide costs or next steps.

**Risk reversal.** State only policies that exist, in their actual terms: a real trial, a real refund window, "no card required," "cancel anytime." Put the reversal next to the ask. If there is no policy, do not write a guarantee; flag `[NEED: policy]`.

**Social proof.** Real names, real numbers, real conditions. Specific and attributed beats generic praise. Do not write "join thousands" or "trending now" without a source.

**Reciprocity and small steps.** Give something useful before a hard ask (a sample, a guide, a working free tier). Ask for a first step the reader can actually take.

**Scarcity and urgency.** Only ship a constraint the buyer could verify: cohort dates, genuine capacity, inventory, a scheduled price change you will honor, a bonus that actually expires. No resetting countdown timers, no "3 spots left" on an unconstrained digital product, no "127 people are viewing this," no weekly "last chance" emails. If there is no real constraint, omit scarcity. A weak offer plus fake urgency just gets a faster no.

**Framing.** You may emphasize a true loss of inaction or a true gain, and you may show a real higher price or plan first as an anchor. Do not flip facts, invent a decoy, or imply a discount that is always on.

## Readability

- Prefer clear over clever. If the reader has to decode the line, it fails.
- Body sentences: aim under 20 words; split anything past about 25. Mix short and medium; do not stack same-length sentences.
- Paragraphs: 1–3 sentences in email and social; 2–4 on web pages. Subheads and lists where they help a scanner.
- General-audience page copy should read easily on a first pass (about an 8th-grade level). Go up only for a clearly technical audience; still define uncommon terms at first use.
- Active voice; name the actor. One idea per sentence. State the fact, not a claim that the fact is "crucial" or "seamless."
- Read the draft aloud. Revisit every sentence over 20 words. For web pages, if asked: a clear meta title and a meta description under 160 characters.

## Editing sweeps

For a blank or sloppy draft, or when restoring a real voice, run these passes in order. After each pass, glance back so a later fix has not broken an earlier one. Skip the sweeps for a point edit: return only the changed lines with a one-line reason each, and mention a serious problem elsewhere in one line without fixing it unasked.

1. **Clarity** — immediately understandable; no jargon without a gloss; no sentence doing two jobs.
2. **Voice** — consistent formality and person; still sounds like the same speaker.
3. **So what** — every feature has a benefit the reader can feel; company achievements that do not help the reader get cut or bridged.
4. **Prove it** — every claim has support nearby, or the claim is narrowed or flagged.
5. **Specificity** — replace "improve," "enhance," "many," "fast" with a number, timeframe, or example; drop what cannot be made specific.
6. **Feeling** — the before-state is vivid enough to recognize, without melodrama or fake vulnerability.
7. **Zero risk** — objections, trust, next steps, and any real reversal sit next to the CTA; no hidden surprise.

Leave-it-alone test: revert any change you cannot give a reason for. The core message should survive every pass.

## Slop score

Rate the draft 1–10 on each. Below 35 out of 50, revise before delivery.

| Dimension | Question |
|-----------|----------|
| Directness | Does it state the point, or announce and stage it? |
| Rhythm | Varied, or metronomic (triads, fragments, tidy closers)? |
| Trust | Would a careful reader believe the claims? |
| Authenticity | Does it sound like a person, or like a template? |
| Density | Can anything be cut without losing meaning? |

Search for the banned patterns above before handing over the draft. Do not swap synonyms in a way that creates a new tell. Missing proof stays `[NEED: ...]`.

## Pre-publish checklist

- Does the first screen state the offer and the strongest true advantage? Does "Now you can…" still hold?
- Is every claim supportable? Any invented stat, quote, guarantee, or fake scarcity?
- Any self-weakening phrase, apology, or case made for the other side?
- Any process diary, buried lead, or feature with no "so what"?
- Any AI tell: not-X-but-Y, negation lists, throat-clearing, stock phrases, fake authority, tidy closers, broetry, fake "Re:"?
- Is the primary CTA specific about what the reader gets, and is the next step obvious?
- Are real objections and any real risk reversal next to the ask?
- Could a competitor use this copy unchanged? If yes, add the real differentiator or flag it.
- Readability: long sentences split, tone consistent, slop score at least 35/50?
- Point edit only: did you change more than you were asked to?
